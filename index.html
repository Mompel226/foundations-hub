Mitochondria: an oval or a tube?

Both are right. A book draws a mitochondrion as an oval, cut open so that you can see inside it. This zoom shows the mitochondria of one real cell, measured by an electron microscope: many of them are long tubes. Here is why the two look different.

Step 1 of 7

Loading the two mitochondria…

In this cell

There are 351 mitochondria. 7 in 10 are short, under 2 µm long: these look like the drawing in a book. 3 in 10 are longer tubes, from 2 µm up to 14 µm long, and they hold three quarters of all the mitochondria. Mitochondria also join together and divide again all the time, so their shapes change.

So the drawing in a book shows one short mitochondrion, cut open lengthways, so that you can see its two membranes and the folds (cristae). A photograph from an electron microscope shows one thin slice of the cell: in it, short and long mitochondria all look like ovals or circles.

In your exam

Exam questions use drawings and photographs like the ones in your book. Name a mitochondrion by what it has, not by its whole shape: two membranes, and the inner one folded (cristae).

Measured in this cell (OpenOrganelle, jrc_hela-2). The two mitochondria in the steps, their outlines and the photograph are real: the outlines are the computer's own, from the same data as the 3D. The slice straight across the tube is imagined; its outline is worked out from the real tube. Others found the same with slices one after another: what looked like many separate mitochondria in yeast is one branched mitochondrion (Hoffmann and Avers, Science, 1973); the mitochondria of a muscle cell form a joined-up network (Glancy and others, Nature 523:617, 2015); mitochondria join and divide (Westermann, Nature Reviews Molecular Cell Biology 11:872, 2010).

About this picture

  • Real: the woman's body and organs (a medical atlas), the tissue photographs, and the cell and its organelles, measured by an electron microscope.
  • A model: the tissue, the inside of the cut organ, the cell rising from the tissue, and the molecules. Their sizes are real; where each cell and molecule sits is made up. The kinesin walks 100 times slower than in life.
  • Three different people: the body, the tissue and the cell. The cell is a HeLa cell, grown in a laboratory.
  • Colours are chosen: most parts of a cell are too small to have a colour. The nucleus is violet at every level.
  • Not shown: water, most proteins and ribosomes (far too many), and movement: the cell was frozen.
